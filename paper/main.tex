\section{Introduction}


\section{Background}

\subsection{Rendering with Gaussian splats}
\label{sec:rendering-with-splats}

Neural radiance fields represent density and view-dependent colour as
continuous functions and render pixels by integrating radiance along camera
rays \cite{mildenhall2020}. 3DGS instead represents the scene explicitly as
anisotropic Gaussian primitives \cite{kerbl2023}. Each primitive $i$ has a
centre $\mu_i$, covariance $\Sigma_i$, opacity parameter, and a
view-dependent colour $c_i(d)$, commonly represented by spherical harmonics.

For a pixel $q$, the visible Gaussians are projected to the image plane and
sorted from front to back. If $\alpha_{q,i}$ is the projected opacity of the
$i$th primitive and

\begin{equation}
    T_{q,i}=\prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_{q,j})
\end{equation}

is its accumulated transmittance, the rendered colour is

\begin{equation}
    C(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{m} T_{q,i}\alpha_{q,i}c_i(d_q).
    \label{eq:alpha-compositing}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:alpha-compositing} is a weighted sum, but it is also a
quadrature rule. It evaluates the continuous volume-rendering functional

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_q[c]=\int a_q(\xi)c(\xi,d_q)\,d\xi,
    \label{eq:rendering-functional}
\end{equation}

where $a_q(\xi)$ combines transmittance, opacity or density, and the projected
footprint of the ray. Under the piecewise-constant colour and density model
used in volume rendering, the integral reduces exactly to the ordered
alpha-compositing sum \cite{max1995}. Thus compositing is not merely analogous
to quadrature: it is a particular quadrature rule for the rendering integral,
with nodes supplied by the primitives and weights supplied by the renderer.

\subsection{From quadrature rules to uncertainty}

Consider a general integral

\begin{equation}
    I[f]=\int_{\Omega} f(x)p(x)\,dx,
    \label{eq:integral}
\end{equation}

where $f$ is known only at locations $X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_m\}$. A quadrature
rule approximates this integral by

\begin{equation}
    I[f]\approx \widehat I[f]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_i f(x_i).
    \label{eq:quadrature}
\end{equation}

The nodes $x_i$ determine where the function is evaluated and the weights
$w_i$ determine how those values contribute. Newton--Cotes, Gaussian
quadrature, Monte Carlo integration, and alpha compositing all take this form;
they differ in how they choose nodes and weights. A conventional quadrature
rule returns an estimate but does not, by itself, express what could have
happened between the samples.

Probabilistic numerics treats that missing information as an inference
problem. In Bayesian quadrature \cite{ohagan1991,briol2019}, the unknown
integrand is assigned a Gaussian-process prior,

\begin{equation}
    f\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k),
\end{equation}

where $k$ encodes assumptions about the variation of $f$. Conditioning on
$\mathbf{f}=[f(x_1),\ldots,f(x_m)]^\top$ produces a posterior over functions.
Since integration is linear, the induced posterior over the integral is
Gaussian:

\begin{equation}
    I[f]\mid \mathbf f,X \sim
    \mathcal N\!\left(\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf f,
    z_0-\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf z\right),
    \label{eq:bq-posterior}
\end{equation}

with

\begin{align}
    K_{ij} &= k(x_i,x_j), \\
    z_i &= \int_{\Omega}k(x,x_i)p(x)\,dx, \\
    z_0 &= \int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}
    k(x,x')p(x)p(x')\,dx\,dx'.
\end{align}

The posterior mean is itself a quadrature rule, with weights
$\mathbf w=K^{-1}\mathbf z$. The posterior variance

\begin{equation}
    u(\Omega)=z_0-\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf z
    \label{eq:variance}
\end{equation}

depends on the sample locations, integration measure, and kernel, but not on
the observed function values. It is small when the samples constrain all
functions allowed by the prior over the relevant domain. It is large when
substantially different functions can pass through the same observations yet
produce different integrals. This is the sense in which Bayesian quadrature
provides epistemic uncertainty about a numerical computation.

The same quantity has a deterministic interpretation. For functions in the
reproducing-kernel Hilbert space associated with $k$,

\begin{equation}
    |I[f]-\widehat I[f]|
    \leq \lVert f\rVert_k\sqrt{u(\Omega)}.
    \label{eq:error-bound}
\end{equation}

The posterior standard deviation is therefore also the worst-case integration
error over the unit ball of that function space.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Uncertainty in radiance fields and Gaussian splatting}

Uncertainty methods for radiance fields and 3DGS broadly operate on model
parameters, rendered predictions, or observation coverage; a recent taxonomy
organizes the field along these lines \cite{klasson2024sources}. Stochastic
Gaussian Splatting learns distributions over Gaussian parameters using
variational inference \cite{savant2024}. Bayes' Rays applies a Laplace
approximation to a deformation field \cite{goli2024}. FisherRF uses Fisher
information for uncertainty estimation and active view selection
\cite{jiang2024}, while PUP 3D-GS uses second-order sensitivity for pruning
\cite{hanson2025}. GAVIS records directional visibility using spherical
harmonics for active mapping \cite{xue2026}. Mip-Splatting addresses a related
but distinct source of error: aliasing under changes in image sampling rate
\cite{yu2024}.

Two lines of concurrent work put a per-primitive spherical-harmonic
representation on uncertainty itself, rather than on colour. Galappaththige
et al.\ freeze a trained map and \emph{learn} an additional per-primitive
uncertainty channel from training-view reconstruction residuals
\cite{galappaththige2026predictive}: the channel is rendered with the same
alpha-compositing weights as colour, and a Gaussian prior (implemented as
$L^2$ regularisation) drives unsupported directions towards a prescribed
maximum. Their channel is trained to predict the observed photometric
\emph{residual}, which is a different quantity from a posterior over the
colour coefficients themselves. Han and Dumery similarly give each Gaussian
an SH-valued uncertainty field, but fit it with a hand-designed
``back-to-back'' loss that pushes uncertainty down along observed viewing
directions and up along the opposing ones \cite{han2025viewdependent}. Both
require an optimisation pass after the map is frozen. The construction in
Section~\ref{sec:method} differs from both in kind, not just in loss: the
per-splat coefficient posterior's precision (Eq.~\eqref{eq:sh-precision}) is
read off directly as a closed-form Fisher/coverage statistic of the frozen
map -- how strongly, and from which directions, each splat was already
constrained by training -- so no channel is trained, tuned, or regularised
into existence; only a Cholesky solve and a render.

Sampling posterior renders through an otherwise-unmodified rasterizer to get
predictive uncertainty, the strategy in Section~\ref{sec:method}, also
appears concurrently in two settings that put the posterior over a different
part of the model. Jia et al.\ track Gaussian \emph{geometry} (means and
covariances) with a Normal-Inverse-Wishart posterior fit jointly with
training and re-render posterior geometry samples for predictive intervals
and active view selection \cite{jia2026rendering}; Wu et al.\ apply the same
render-the-ensemble idea to per-Gaussian \emph{density} for sparse-view X-ray
CT reconstruction with 3DGS \cite{wu2026perturbed}. Neither targets the
appearance coefficients a photometric posterior needs, and Section~\ref{sec:method}'s
own attempt to add a geometry (opacity) term to an otherwise appearance-only
posterior made calibration worse, not better -- an entirely fixed,
frozen-map construction and an in-the-loop geometric one are not
interchangeable, and which one helps is not obvious a priori. Horseshoe
Splatting instead puts a sparsity-inducing prior on per-splat \emph{covariance}
structure and fits the whole posterior variationally during training
\cite{wu2026horseshoe}, again a geometric rather than photometric target and,
unlike the post-hoc construction here, not separable from the training
procedure.

Closest in spirit to Section~\ref{sec:renderer-consistency}'s central move
-- exploit the renderer's exact linearity in the per-primitive unknowns to
get a closed form the unmodified rasterizer verifies by construction,
instead of solving a directional Bayesian-quadrature system that does not
track it -- is concurrent work in a different modality. Zhao et al.\ derive
a closed-form posterior variance for radiative (X-ray) Gaussian splatting by
using the strict linearity of X-ray attenuation in per-Gaussian density, and
report it as the exact infinite-sample limit of an ensemble/sampling
estimator at roughly $8\times$ lower cost \cite{zhao2026posterior}. Their
finding that this variance is a data-\emph{constraint} map rather than an
error map -- it ranks true error well overall but the ranking collapses well
below chance inside the reconstructed object, where whole-frame correlation
is dominated by the object/background split -- is close to the finding in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} that whole-frame Spearman correlation on
NeRF-Synthetic scenes is driven by the same silhouette split, not by
calibration on the object itself; that this shows up in two independent
constructions, in two different modalities (X-ray density and SH colour),
is evidence it is a structural property of renderer-linear closed-form
posteriors rather than an artifact of either implementation.

Finally, on the question of whether a fitted correction transfers across
scenes without held-out views, Chu et al.\ propose View-Structured Conformal
Prediction, which factors a pre-calibration scale into a renderer-derived
spatial shape and a transferable scalar view-difficulty multiplier, and
report a held-out per-view quantile that gives finite-sample coverage even
when transferred to a new scene \cite{chu2026conformal}. That is a
distribution-free guarantee on coverage; the anchoring in
Section~\ref{sec:kernel-choice} instead transfers two dimensionless
constants of a parametric NLL fit by tying the aleatoric floor to each
scene's own training residual. The two are not mutually exclusive and answer
related but different questions -- a certified coverage guarantee versus a
calibrated predictive density -- and a conformal wrapper around the
posterior here is a natural next comparison rather than a competitor to rule
out.


\subsection{Bayesian quadrature for representation analysis}

Bayesian quadrature is usually used when function evaluations are expensive
and uncertainty in an integral matters \cite{ohagan1991,briol2019}. Our use is
slightly different. The function values stored by a trained 3DGS map are
already available; the question is whether their locations provide adequate
support for a new spatial or directional query. The posterior variance is
therefore used as a geometric property of the representation. This turns a
tool for uncertain numerical integration into a post-hoc diagnostic for
finite scene maps.

\section{A Renderer-Consistent Posterior over Splat Appearance}
\label{sec:method}

Building on Section~\ref{sec:rendering-with-splats}'s observation that
alpha compositing is a quadrature rule, the natural first attempt is to put
a Bayesian-quadrature posterior directly on the rendering integral, with a
joint position--direction kernel, and solve for the Bayes--Hermite-optimal
weights. Section~\ref{sec:retired-directional-kernel} records why that
attempt was retired, because the failure motivates every choice below.
Section~\ref{sec:renderer-consistency} then states the property the
construction must have; Sections~\ref{sec:posterior}--\ref{sec:rademacher}
give the posterior actually used, over each splat's own stored
spherical-harmonic coefficients, with its precision read directly off the
renderer's own compositing weights; Section~\ref{sec:ensemble} pushes it
forward by rendering samples from it; Section~\ref{sec:kernel-choice} fits
the remaining constants and calibrates the result.

\subsection{Why a point-evaluation directional kernel fails}
\label{sec:retired-directional-kernel}

For a position-only kernel and point-mass primitives, the Bayesian-quadrature-\emph{optimal}
solved-for weights $\mathbf w^\ast=K^{-1}\mathbf z$ coincide exactly with
the real compositing weights $w_{q,i}=T_{q,i}\alpha_{q,i}$, for any kernel
bandwidth. That equivalence breaks the moment a directional term is
added to the kernel to say anything about viewing-angle coverage: the
query moment vector evaluates direction against one fixed query direction,
$k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d_i,d_*)$, while the Gram matrix correlates direction
between candidate pairs, $k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d_i,d_j)$. These enter the
linear system in structurally different ways and do not cancel, so the
solved-for weights $\mathbf w^\ast$ no longer track $w_{q,i}$ once
direction is involved --- confirmed directly (exact agreement as
$\kappa\to0$, disagreement of two orders of magnitude at a realistic
$\kappa$). Visually, this appears as chromatic speckle in the
Bayesian-quadrature posterior mean: a directional Bayesian-quadrature
\emph{mean} is a worse image than the alpha-compositing render it is
supposed to refine.

The fix is not a better directional kernel. A splat's stored
spherical-harmonic coefficients are not a point observation of the
radiance field at a location and direction; they are the parameters
photometric training already fit. Treating them as the unknowns of a
regression problem, rather than as point evaluations to be explained by a
\emph{solved} quadrature rule, sidesteps the cancellation above entirely,
because no linear system is ever solved for the posterior mean.

\subsection{The renderer as an exact posterior mean}
\label{sec:renderer-consistency}

Fix the splat geometry (centres, covariances, opacities) and, for one
colour channel, let $\theta_i\in\R^{B}$ collect splat $i$'s spherical-
harmonic coefficients, with real SH feature vector $\phi(d)\in\R^{B}$ (the
same basis \texttt{gsplat}/3DGS evaluate a splat's colour against, pulled
out as a design vector rather than evaluated straight through a fixed
coefficient vector). Splat $i$'s colour in direction $d$ is
$c_i(d)=\phi(d)^\top\theta_i$. Writing the real compositing weight as
$\beta_{q,i}=T_{q,i}\alpha_{q,i}$ and stacking every splat's coefficients
into $\theta$, alpha compositing (Eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha-compositing}) is
exactly the linear read-out

\begin{equation}
    C_\alpha(q)=\sum_i\beta_{q,i}\,\phi(d_q)^\top\theta_i
    = b_q^\top\theta,
    \qquad
    b_q^\top=\big[\beta_{q,1}\phi(d_q)^\top,\ldots\big].
    \label{eq:renderer-readout}
\end{equation}

Because Eq.~\eqref{eq:renderer-readout} is linear in $\theta$, mean
preservation needs no special machinery: for \emph{any} distribution over
$\theta$ with mean $\hat\theta$ (the coefficients 3DGS actually trained),
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E\big[C_\alpha(q)\big] = b_q^\top\mathbb E[\theta]
    = b_q^\top\hat\theta = C_\alpha(q)\big|_{\theta=\hat\theta}
    \label{eq:mean-preservation}
\end{equation}
for every query $q$, exactly. This is the property Section~\ref
{sec:retired-directional-kernel}'s construction never delivers once
direction is involved, because it \emph{solves} for a mean instead of
anchoring one directly: there, $\mathbf w^\ast$ is the solution of a linear
system that direction perturbs away from $w_{q,i}$. Here the mean is never
solved for. It is fixed at the trained checkpoint by construction, and only
the spread around it is estimated, so uncertainty is strictly post hoc --
a property added on top of a render that Eq.~\eqref{eq:mean-preservation}
leaves untouched -- and cannot corrupt the reconstruction, whatever
covariance is placed on $\theta$.

\subsection{A closed-form posterior from the renderer's own weights}
\label{sec:posterior}

We place a Gaussian prior on each splat's coefficients,
$\theta_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\Lambda^{-1})$, and treat $\hat\theta_i$ as the
MAP point a Laplace approximation is built around --- in the spirit of
Bayes' Rays \cite{goli2024}, but for the appearance coefficients of a
frozen splat map rather than a deformation field, and without retraining
to reach that MAP point: it is simply what photometric training already
found. The approximate posterior covariance is the inverse curvature of
the negative log-posterior at $\hat\theta_i$ --- a Gauss--Newton/Fisher
approximation built from the renderer's own Jacobian
$\partial C_\alpha(q)/\partial\theta_i=\beta_{q,i}\phi(d_q)^\top$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:renderer-readout}) and a homoscedastic observation-noise
variance $\sigma_n^2$:
\begin{equation}
    P_i = \Lambda + \frac{1}{\sigma_n^2}\,D_i,
    \qquad
    D_i = \sum_{p\in\mathcal D_i}\Big(\sum_q \beta_{q,i,p}^2\Big)\,
    \phi(d_{i,p})\phi(d_{i,p})^\top,
    \label{eq:sh-precision}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \theta_i \mid \mathcal D \;\approx\; \mathcal N\big(\hat\theta_i,\,
    \Sigma_{\theta_i}\big), \qquad \Sigma_{\theta_i} = P_i^{-1},
    \label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal D_i$ is the set of real training cameras splat $i$
plausibly observed and $\beta_{q,i,p}$ is its real compositing weight at
pixel $q$ of training image $p$. Crucially, $\sum_q\beta_{q,i,p}^2$ sums
over \emph{every} pixel the splat's real footprint touches in that
training image, not just the pixel its projected centre lands on: a
training observation constrains a splat through its whole footprint, and a
splat covering 400 pixels carries far more information per observation
than one covering one pixel. An earlier surrogate that sampled $\beta$ only
at the projected centre, from a fixed-radius candidate ball treated as a
binary occluder mask, understated this quantity by a median factor of
$2.1\times10^{18}$ on a real 300{,}000-splat checkpoint --- numerically
zero --- which made every posterior in that surrogate collapse exactly onto
its prior. $\Sigma_{\theta_i}$ depends only on which directions splat $i$
was observed from and how strongly each observation weighted it, never on
the observed \emph{colours}: it is a Fisher-information/coverage
statistic. A splat seen from many angularly diverse, strongly-weighted
directions has small $\Sigma_{\theta_i}$ everywhere; one seen from a narrow
cone, or only ever weakly, stays uncertain away from that cone.

\subsection{Computing $D_i$ from the rasterizer itself}
\label{sec:rademacher}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:sh-precision} needs $\sum_q\beta_{q,i,p}^2$ for every splat
and every training camera. \texttt{gsplat} does not expose $\beta$
directly, but it does render $I(q)=\sum_i\beta_{q,i}c_i$ for an arbitrary
per-splat scalar feature $c_i$ substituted in place of colour, so
backpropagating an image-space loss $r$ through the unmodified rasterizer
gives, by the chain rule,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial c_i}\sum_q r_q I(q) = \sum_q \beta_{q,i}\,r_q,
    \label{eq:rademacher-grad}
\end{equation}
exactly, at the cost of one backward pass. Taking $r_q$ to be independent
Rademacher ($\pm1$) noise, $\mathbb E\big[(\sum_q\beta_{q,i}r_q)^2\big]
=\sum_q\beta_{q,i}^2$ --- Hutchinson's trace-estimator trick, applied here
to extract the exact per-splat quantity Eq.~\eqref{eq:sh-precision} needs
from the rasterizer's own autodiff graph, with no custom kernel and no
approximation of $\beta$ by a bearing-ball or KD-tree candidate search.
Because $\beta$ is purely geometric (it does not depend on which feature
$c_i$ carries), independent probes ride as independent \emph{colour
channels} of a single render, so several probes --- enough to average
$\sum_q\beta_{q,i}^2$ down to low variance --- cost one forward-plus-backward
pass per training camera, not one pass per probe. On a 300{,}000-splat,
100-camera checkpoint this accumulation takes 5.0\,s and gives a data/prior
precision ratio at the $\ell=0$ (DC colour) band with median 25.7 and 85\%
of splats data-dominated, against $10^{-13}$ and 0\% for the retired
surrogate at $45\times$ the wall-clock cost.

\subsection{Posterior-ensemble rendering}
\label{sec:ensemble}

The predictive pixel distribution induced by Eq.~\eqref{eq:posterior} has a
closed form under a cross-splat independence approximation,
$u(q)=\sum_i\beta_{q,i}^2\,\phi(d_q)^\top\Sigma_{\theta_i}\phi(d_q)$, but we
do not evaluate it as a quadratic form. The cheapest and most legible way
to push a posterior through a quadrature rule is to sample from it: draw
$\theta^{(s)}\sim\mathcal N(\hat\theta,\Sigma_\theta)$ for $s=1,\ldots,k$
and render each draw with the real, unmodified rasterizer. The per-pixel
sample spread across the $k$ renders is the uncertainty; the sample mean
recovers $C_\alpha(q)$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:mean-preservation}. No
matrix-vector product against $b_q$, no explicit $\Sigma_\theta$, and no
retraining are needed --- just a one-time Cholesky factorization of each
$P_i$ (306\,ms for a 300{,}000-splat scene) and $k$ extra forward renders
($k=8$ costs 323\,ms against a 21.5\,ms single render).

We also tried extending this posterior beyond appearance: sampling the
\emph{joint} posterior over every splat's coefficients without the
block-diagonal independence assumption (matrix-free, via the same
backward-pass matvec trick, solved by preconditioned CG), and adding
opacity as a second, geometric term under the same Laplace construction.
Both are implemented and validated but did not improve calibration on real
checkpoints (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}); the appearance-only,
block-diagonal posterior above is the one used throughout.

\subsection{Prior, noise, and calibration}
\label{sec:kernel-choice}

$\Lambda$ is fit by empirical Bayes directly from the checkpoint's own
coefficient population, per spherical-harmonic band $\ell$ and colour
channel $c$: $\lambda_{\ell,c}=1/\mathrm{Var}_i[\theta_{i,c,k}: k\in
\mathrm{band}\ \ell]$. A single scalar prior is badly mis-specified
regardless of any other choice: on the lego \texttt{wide} checkpoint, the
fitted $\ell=0$ (DC colour) precision is 1.55/1.92/3.48 (RGB) against
$\ell=2$'s 10.6--11.0, an order of magnitude apart. $\sigma_n^2$ is fit as
the mean squared residual between the real render and the real training
images on the checkpoint's own training views (0.0129 for lego
\texttt{wide}) --- no held-out data is used anywhere in fitting the
posterior itself.

The raw posterior from Eq.~\eqref{eq:posterior} is well-ranked but
under-confident by a roughly constant factor: binning predicted $\sigma$
against the RMS residual actually observed in each bin gives a ratio that
is nearly flat across a decade of $\sigma$, i.e.\ the \emph{shape} is right
and one scalar is wrong. We fit that scalar, plus an additive floor for the
error a posterior over fitted parameters structurally cannot see
(misspecification, resolution limits), on held-in views and score on
held-out ones:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\mathrm{total}}^2(q) = s^2\,u(q) + \sigma_0^2.
    \label{eq:calibration}
\end{equation}
Fit this way per scene, $s$ is 5.5 (lego \texttt{wide}) and 6.4 (lego
\texttt{gap\_4}, Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) --- the raw posterior is
underconfident by a factor of about 5--6 on two very different
checkpoints. Across all seven NeRF-Synthetic scenes, $s$ is close to
constant (mean 4.97, s.d.\ 0.72) while $\sigma_0$ varies by up to
$9.8\times$, tracking how hard each scene is to reconstruct rather than
any property of the construction. Anchoring the floor to each scene's own
training residual, $\sigma_0=c\,\sigma_n$ with a single transferred
$c\approx1.8$, and transferring $s\approx5.0$ as well, requires no
held-out views on a new scene at all and recovers 66\% of the calibration
gain a per-scene fit would give (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\section{Using the Uncertainty}

\subsection{Uncertainty maps}

We form uncertainty images by drawing $k$ posterior samples
(Section~\ref{sec:ensemble}) and rendering each through \texttt{gsplat} at
every real ray--surface hit in a held-out view, reporting the renderer's
own output $C_\alpha(q)$ (never a solved-for mean) alongside the per-pixel
sample standard deviation and, once fit, the calibrated
$\sigma_{\mathrm{total}}(q)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:calibration}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

All experiments use real \texttt{gsplat} training, real checkpoints and
real cameras on NeRF-Synthetic scenes; no synthetic stand-in for $\beta$ or
the coefficient posterior is used anywhere.

\subsection{The mean never departs from the renderer}

$C_\alpha(q)$ in Figure~\ref{fig:posterior-ensemble} is, by construction
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:mean-preservation}), the real \texttt{gsplat} output. Every
posterior draw is visually near-identical to it on the fully-observed
checkpoint, with none of the chromatic speckle the retired directional
point-evaluation kernel produced (Section~\ref{sec:retired-directional-kernel}).

\subsection{Spread tracks how much data the posterior is conditioned on}

Figure~\ref{fig:view-sweep} freezes one checkpoint's geometry, opacities,
coefficients, query camera and render, and varies only how many of its own
real training cameras $\Sigma_\theta$ is conditioned on --- strictly
nested, with no retraining confound of any kind. Per-pixel std on the
object rises monotonically as the conditioning set shrinks
(Table~\ref{tab:view-sweep}): a correctness property (nested conditioning
can only drop positive semi-definite terms from $D_i$, so posterior
variance cannot decrease as cameras are removed) and a sanity result.

\subsection{Per-view calibration in the epistemic regime}

A posterior over fitted parameters measures \emph{epistemic} uncertainty
--- what training failed to determine --- so it should track held-out
error best when that error is actually epistemic. We test this on three
\emph{retrained} checkpoints, each with a deliberate angular hole of
increasing width in its training views (retraining, not frozen-map
conditioning, is required here, since a frozen map's reconstruction error
does not change when the posterior is conditioned on fewer of the same
cameras). Table~\ref{tab:epistemic} and Figure~\ref{fig:epistemic} score
per-view mean predicted std against per-view mean held-out error over 30
eval views per checkpoint.

\subsection{Per-pixel calibration, scored against its own attainable ceiling}

A whole-frame Spearman correlation between predicted std and $|$error$|$
that includes background pixels reaches 0.95 on these scenes, but that is
almost entirely the object/background silhouette split, since both are
near zero on background; every number below is restricted to object
pixels. Rank correlation between a predicted $\sigma$ and a single noise
realization $|\epsilon|=\sigma|z|$ is, even restricted that way, bounded
well below 1 for a perfectly calibrated $\sigma$, since $|z|$ alone injects
$\mathrm{Var}(\log|z|)=\pi^2/8$ of irreducible rank noise. We therefore
score observed Spearman against the ceiling the same $\sigma_{\mathrm
{pred}}$ would attain if it were exactly correct (simulating
$\epsilon^\ast\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}^2)$ and re-running the
identical metric), fitting Eq.~\eqref{eq:calibration} on half the held-out
views and scoring on the other half (Table~\ref{tab:pixel-calibration},
Figure~\ref{fig:pixel-calibration}).

In the epistemic regime the per-pixel ranking is 98\% of everything a
perfectly calibrated uncertainty could achieve; on a checkpoint fit to 100

absent (+0.68 nats when there is real epistemic error to find) --- the
correct behaviour of an epistemic posterior, not a deficiency of it.

\subsection{Calibration transfers across scenes, but only when anchored}

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} and Figure~\ref{fig:transfer} fit
Eq.~\eqref{eq:calibration} on all seven NeRF-Synthetic scenes,
leave-one-scene-out. $s$ (mean 4.97, s.d.\ 0.72) is close to a property of
the construction; $\sigma_0$ (up to $9.8\times$ apart) is not --- it is
each scene's own irreducible misspecification level. Anchoring
$\sigma_0=c\,\sigma_n$ to the scene's own training residual $\sigma_n$
(already needed to fit $D_i$, Section~\ref{sec:kernel-choice}; no held-out
views) transfers only two dimensionless numbers ($s\approx5.0$,
$c\approx1.8$) and recovers 66\% of the gap between naive transfer and a
full per-scene fit; on two scenes (hotdog, lego) it beats the per-scene fit
outright, because pooling across six scenes regularizes constants a
15-view per-scene fit overfits. The remaining outlier is \texttt{mic}, a
largely specular object whose training residual under-predicts held-out
error more than any other scene.

\subsection{Richer posteriors that did not help}

Two extensions from Section~\ref{sec:ensemble} were tested against real
held-out error and rejected on that basis, not on cost alone.
\textbf{Cross-splat coupling} (Table~\ref{tab:coupling},
Figure~\ref{fig:coupling}) raises per-pixel std by a median factor of 1.13
at $1000\times$ the sampling cost and makes object-pixel Spearman against
held-out error slightly \emph{worse}: overlapping splats are jointly
non-identifiable and anti-correlated along a ray, so coupling widens each
splat's own marginal uncertainty while the rendered sum --- what is
actually scored --- is already well determined by the block-diagonal
approximation. \textbf{Putting opacity into the same Laplace construction}
(mean over three held-out lego views, object pixels) drops Spearman from
0.265 (appearance only) to 0.170 (opacity only) to 0.152 (both), because
occluded splats accumulate posterior std equal to the prior --- entirely
unconstrained --- and perturbing them by that much in logit space can turn
a buried splat into a visible occluder, an effect a linearized Fisher
approximation cannot see. Both results are consistent with the same
reading: the appearance-only, block-diagonal posterior is not leaving
calibration on the table by being simple.
