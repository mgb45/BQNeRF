\begin{abstract}
Three-dimensional Gaussian splatting reconstructs a scene as a finite set of
Gaussian primitives and renders new views by alpha compositing their projected
contributions. The rendered image, however, does not indicate whether a query
is supported by many observations or extrapolated from sparse spatial or
directional coverage. We introduce \method{}, a post-hoc uncertainty measure
for trained Gaussian-splat maps. Our key observation is that rendering is a
quadrature problem: a continuous radiance integral is evaluated from a finite
set of primitives. We place a Gaussian-process prior on the unknown scene
function and use Bayesian quadrature to obtain a posterior distribution over
the corresponding integral. Its variance depends on the locations and viewing
directions of the available splats, and therefore measures representation
support without an ensemble, an auxiliary uncertainty network, or retraining.
We also derive a rendering-aware kernel by incorporating the query-specific
alpha-compositing weights into the covariance function. This connects the
quadrature variance directly to the rendered quantity and suppresses the
influence of occluded primitives. Held-out reconstruction quality and quadrature uncertainty both track the
number of primitives allotted to a scene: sweeping a single scene from 500 to
1,000,000 splats raises held-out PSNR from $21.4$ to $41.0\,$dB while
posterior variance visibly concentrates from covering nearly the whole object
down to a handful of under-resolved regions. A controlled coverage experiment
shows the same posterior variance increasing monotonically, by roughly two
orders of magnitude, as training viewing directions are removed. \method{}
measures how strongly a finite scene representation
supports a query; it complements methods that learn to predict photometric
error from reconstruction residuals.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A scene representation used by a robot must do more than render plausible
images. It must also indicate where those images are supported by evidence. A
surface reconstructed from many nearby observations and a surface inferred
from a narrow range of views may look equally convincing, even though the
second is a much riskier basis for planning, inspection, or interaction.

Three-dimensional Gaussian splatting (3DGS) makes this problem especially
clear. A trained map stores a finite collection of Gaussian primitives. To
render a pixel, these primitives are projected into the image, ordered in
depth, and alpha composited. The renderer always returns a colour, but standard
3DGS does not report whether the relevant part of position--view space was
dense enough to constrain that result.

This paper approaches the problem through numerical integration. Rendering is
the evaluation of an integral using finitely many samples. Classical
quadrature provides a weighted approximation to that integral, while Bayesian
quadrature places a probability distribution over the unknown integrand and,
therefore, over the integral itself. The resulting posterior variance measures
how much freedom remains between the available samples. Dense, well-spread
samples reduce this freedom; sparse or clustered samples do not.

This interpretation leads directly to \method{}. We treat splat centres as
spatial samples of a local scene function and observed camera directions as
samples of its angular dependence. A Gaussian-process kernel specifies how
information transfers between nearby positions and directions. Bayesian
quadrature then gives a closed-form uncertainty measure for each query. The
method is post-hoc: it operates on an existing Gaussian-splat map and does not
fit a second predictor to held-out errors.

The rendering operation also determines a particular kernel structure. For a
query ray $q$, each point is weighted by opacity, visibility, and accumulated
transmittance. Multiplying a base scene kernel by these rendering weights on
both arguments produces a query-specific covariance. Equivalently, one can
retain the base kernel and integrate it against the alpha-compositing measure.
Both views yield the same Bayesian-quadrature moments. The resulting variance
describes uncertainty in the rendered integral rather than in an arbitrary
local average.

Our contribution is therefore not another learned predictor of image error.
It is a probabilistic interpretation of the rendering rule that exposes how
well the finite representation supports a query. Specifically, we:

\begin{itemize}
    \item formulate spatial support in a trained Gaussian-splat map as a
    Bayesian-quadrature problem;
    \item extend the construction to joint position--direction space, so that
    geometric support and view coverage can be examined separately;
    \item derive a rendering-aware kernel from the alpha-compositing weights,
    connecting posterior variance to the same integral evaluated by the
    renderer; and
    \item demonstrate the resulting uncertainty on spatial and directional
    coverage tests.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\subsection{Rendering with Gaussian splats}

Neural radiance fields represent density and view-dependent colour as
continuous functions and render pixels by integrating radiance along camera
rays \cite{mildenhall2020}. 3DGS instead represents the scene explicitly as
anisotropic Gaussian primitives \cite{kerbl2023}. Each primitive $i$ has a
centre $\mu_i$, covariance $\Sigma_i$, opacity parameter, and a
view-dependent colour $c_i(d)$, commonly represented by spherical harmonics.

For a pixel $q$, the visible Gaussians are projected to the image plane and
sorted from front to back. If $\alpha_{q,i}$ is the projected opacity of the
$i$th primitive and

\begin{equation}
    T_{q,i}=\prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_{q,j})
\end{equation}

is its accumulated transmittance, the rendered colour is

\begin{equation}
    C(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{m} T_{q,i}\alpha_{q,i}c_i(d_q).
    \label{eq:alpha-compositing}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:alpha-compositing} is a weighted sum, but it is also a
quadrature rule. It evaluates the continuous volume-rendering functional

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_q[c]=\int a_q(\xi)c(\xi,d_q)\,d\xi,
    \label{eq:rendering-functional}
\end{equation}

where $a_q(\xi)$ combines transmittance, opacity or density, and the projected
footprint of the ray. Under the piecewise-constant colour and density model
used in volume rendering, the integral reduces exactly to the ordered
alpha-compositing sum \cite{max1995}. Thus compositing is not merely analogous
to quadrature: it is a particular quadrature rule for the rendering integral,
with nodes supplied by the primitives and weights supplied by the renderer.

\subsection{From quadrature rules to uncertainty}

Consider a general integral

\begin{equation}
    I[f]=\int_{\Omega} f(x)p(x)\,dx,
    \label{eq:integral}
\end{equation}

where $f$ is known only at locations $X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_m\}$. A quadrature
rule approximates this integral by

\begin{equation}
    I[f]\approx \widehat I[f]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_i f(x_i).
    \label{eq:quadrature}
\end{equation}

The nodes $x_i$ determine where the function is evaluated and the weights
$w_i$ determine how those values contribute. Newton--Cotes, Gaussian
quadrature, Monte Carlo integration, and alpha compositing all take this form;
they differ in how they choose nodes and weights. A conventional quadrature
rule returns an estimate but does not, by itself, express what could have
happened between the samples.

Probabilistic numerics treats that missing information as an inference
problem. In Bayesian quadrature \cite{ohagan1991,briol2019}, the unknown
integrand is assigned a Gaussian-process prior,

\begin{equation}
    f\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k),
\end{equation}

where $k$ encodes assumptions about the variation of $f$. Conditioning on
$\mathbf{f}=[f(x_1),\ldots,f(x_m)]^\top$ produces a posterior over functions.
Since integration is linear, the induced posterior over the integral is
Gaussian:

\begin{equation}
    I[f]\mid \mathbf f,X \sim
    \mathcal N\!\left(\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf f,
    z_0-\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf z\right),
    \label{eq:bq-posterior}
\end{equation}

with

\begin{align}
    K_{ij} &= k(x_i,x_j), \\
    z_i &= \int_{\Omega}k(x,x_i)p(x)\,dx, \\
    z_0 &= \int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}
    k(x,x')p(x)p(x')\,dx\,dx'.
\end{align}

The posterior mean is itself a quadrature rule, with weights
$\mathbf w=K^{-1}\mathbf z$. The posterior variance

\begin{equation}
    u(\Omega)=z_0-\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf z
    \label{eq:variance}
\end{equation}

depends on the sample locations, integration measure, and kernel, but not on
the observed function values. It is small when the samples constrain all
functions allowed by the prior over the relevant domain. It is large when
substantially different functions can pass through the same observations yet
produce different integrals. This is the sense in which Bayesian quadrature
provides epistemic uncertainty about a numerical computation.

The same quantity has a deterministic interpretation. For functions in the
reproducing-kernel Hilbert space associated with $k$,

\begin{equation}
    |I[f]-\widehat I[f]|
    \leq \lVert f\rVert_k\sqrt{u(\Omega)}.
    \label{eq:error-bound}
\end{equation}

The posterior standard deviation is therefore also the worst-case integration
error over the unit ball of that function space.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Uncertainty in radiance fields and Gaussian splatting}

Uncertainty methods for radiance fields and 3DGS broadly operate on model
parameters, rendered predictions, or observation coverage. Stochastic
Gaussian Splatting learns distributions over Gaussian parameters using
variational inference \cite{savant2024}. Bayes' Rays applies a Laplace
approximation to a deformation field \cite{goli2024}. FisherRF uses Fisher
information for uncertainty estimation and active view selection
\cite{jiang2024}, while PUP 3D-GS uses second-order sensitivity for pruning
\cite{hanson2025}. GAVIS records directional visibility using spherical
harmonics for active mapping \cite{xue2026}. Mip-Splatting addresses a related
but distinct source of error: aliasing under changes in image sampling rate
\cite{yu2024}.

Galappaththige et al.\ recently proposed a post-hoc method for predictive
photometric uncertainty in 3DGS \cite{galappaththige2026predictive}. Their
method freezes a trained map and learns an additional per-primitive
uncertainty channel from training-view reconstruction residuals. These
channels are represented with spherical harmonics and rendered with the same
alpha-compositing weights as colour. A Gaussian prior, implemented through
$L^2$ regularisation, drives unsupported directions towards a prescribed
maximum uncertainty.

That method and \method{} are complementary but answer different questions.
Galappaththige et al.\ estimate expected photometric error: their uncertainty
depends on observed reconstruction residuals and can combine epistemic,
aleatoric, and optimisation error. \method{} estimates representation support:
its variance depends on where primitives and observations occur under a
chosen kernel, not on their residual values. Their approach fits extra
spherical-harmonic coefficients using gradient optimisation; ours performs
Gaussian-process conditioning at query time and requires no uncertainty
targets or learned channel. Their output is designed to correlate with pixel
error. Ours instead separates spatial and angular coverage and admits a
Bayesian-quadrature posterior and RKHS error bound. This distinction is useful
in practice: a residual-based predictor can identify a consistently difficult
surface, whereas a support measure can identify an unobserved direction even
when the training residual is small because the model has overfit the
available views.

\subsection{Bayesian quadrature for representation analysis}

Bayesian quadrature is usually used when function evaluations are expensive
and uncertainty in an integral matters \cite{ohagan1991,briol2019}. Our use is
slightly different. The function values stored by a trained 3DGS map are
already available; the question is whether their locations provide adequate
support for a new spatial or directional query. The posterior variance is
therefore used as a geometric property of the representation. This turns a
tool for uncertain numerical integration into a post-hoc diagnostic for
finite scene maps.

\section{Bayesian Quadrature for Gaussian Splat Maps}

\subsection{Spatial support}

Let $\Omega_q\subset\R^3$ be a small region centred on a query point $q$, and
let $X_q=\{x_1,\ldots,x_m\}$ be the centres of nearby splats. We interpret the
splat centres as sample locations of an unknown local scene function. With a
uniform integration density over $\Omega_q$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:variance} gives a
scalar measure of spatial support.

The prior term $z_0$ is the uncertainty in the local integral before any
splats are observed. The term $\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf z$ is the
uncertainty removed by their locations. Nearby splats distributed across the
region produce a large reduction; few, distant, or clustered splats leave a
larger variance. The measure consequently captures sample arrangement rather
than only sample count.

We use an axis-aligned box for $\Omega_q$, retrieve neighbours with a $k$-d
tree, and cap each solve at 400 splats. For separable kernels and box domains,
the required moments factor into one-dimensional integrals. Since spatial
uncertainty uses only the primitive locations, it can be computed from a
trained map without colours, residuals, or additional optimisation.

\subsection{Directional support}

View-dependent appearance requires support in both position and direction. We
represent an observation by $(x_i,d_i)$, where $d_i\in\mathbb S^2$ is a camera
direction from which splat $i$ was observed, and use the product kernel

\begin{equation}
    k\big((x,d),(x',d')\big)
    =k_{\mathrm{pos}}(x,x')k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d,d').
    \label{eq:product-kernel}
\end{equation}

Our directional kernel is

\begin{equation}
    k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d,d')
    =\exp\!\left(\kappa(d^\top d'-1)\right),
    \label{eq:direction-kernel}
\end{equation}

where $\kappa$ controls the angular length scale. Small $\kappa$ transfers
information across a wide range of directions; large $\kappa$ represents
stronger view dependence.

For query direction $d_*$, the kernel mean is

\begin{equation}
    z_i=left[\int_{\Omega_q}k_{\mathrm{pos}}(x,x_i)p(x)\,dx\right]
    k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d_i,d_*).
    \label{eq:directional-mean}
\end{equation}

The resulting variance is low only when the query is supported in both
position and direction. Position-only and joint position--direction
uncertainty are therefore the same construction applied to different input
spaces. We recover camera--splat associations from the training cameras using
frustum intersection and an angular depth test, then attach the corresponding
viewing direction to each visible splat.

\subsection{The rendering-aware kernel}
\label{sec:rendering-aware}

Uniform box quadrature asks whether splats support a local spatial average.
Rendering asks a more specific question: whether they support the weighted
radiance integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:rendering-functional}. Let

\begin{equation}
    a_q(\xi)=T_q(\xi)\,\sigma(\xi)\,G_q(\xi)
    \label{eq:render-weight}
\end{equation}

denote the query-dependent rendering weight, combining accumulated
transmittance, density or opacity, and the projected footprint or visibility
term. There are two equivalent ways to incorporate this structure.

First, place a GP prior on radiance,
$c\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k_{\mathrm{base}})$, and apply the linear rendering
functional $\mathcal L_q$ to that process. The Bayesian-quadrature moments are

\begin{align}
    z_{q,i} &= \int a_q(\xi)
    k_{\mathrm{base}}(\xi,x_i)\,d\xi, \\
    z_{q,0} &= \int\!\int a_q(\xi)a_q(\xi')
    k_{\mathrm{base}}(\xi,\xi')\,d\xi\,d\xi'.
    \label{eq:render-moments}
\end{align}

The observation Gram matrix remains
$K_{ij}=k_{\mathrm{base}}(x_i,x_j)$ because the observations are colours
$c(x_i)$.

Second, define the rendering integrand $g_q(\xi)=a_q(\xi)c(\xi)$. It is also a
Gaussian process, with the query-specific kernel

\begin{equation}
    k_q(\xi,\xi')=
    a_q(\xi)k_{\mathrm{base}}(\xi,\xi')a_q(\xi').
    \label{eq:render-kernel}
\end{equation}

Integrating $g_q$ against the ordinary volume measure gives exactly the same
$z_{q,0}$ as Eq.~\eqref{eq:render-moments}. Equation~\eqref{eq:render-kernel}
is the rendering-aware kernel structure: the renderer gates covariance at
both arguments. Points have little influence when they are occluded, have
negligible projected opacity, or lie outside the pixel footprint. This
equivalence also clarifies an implementation detail: when conditioning on
stored radiance values, use the base Gram matrix and rendering-weighted
moments; when conditioning on values of the weighted integrand, use the full
query-specific Gram matrix.

We approximate $a_q$ by an unnormalised Gaussian whose mass, mean, and
covariance match the contributing splats. For an RBF base kernel, all moments
then follow in closed form from Gaussian-product identities. We consider
three sources of rendering weights: mean local opacity, depth-ordered ray
transmittance, and the anisotropic per-pixel alpha values produced by
\texttt{gsplat}. In the last two cases, the total mass is
$\sum_iT_{q,i}\alpha_{q,i}=1-T_{q,\mathrm{final}}$, matching the standard
alpha-compositing recursion.

The directional extension follows by setting

\begin{equation}
    k_{\mathrm{base}}\big((x,d),(x',d')\big)
    =k_{\mathrm{pos}}(x,x')k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d,d').
\end{equation}

The rendering weight remains spatial: it describes where the ray receives
mass. Directional support enters through the base covariance. Consequently,

\begin{equation}
    z_{q,i}=left[\int a_q(\xi)
    k_{\mathrm{pos}}(\xi,x_i)\,d\xi\right]
    k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d_i,d_*).
    \label{eq:render-directional-mean}
\end{equation}

This separates two distinct reasons for uncertainty: weak contribution along
the ray and poor coverage of the requested viewing direction.

\subsection{Kernel choice and fitting}

The kernel defines which scene functions are plausible between samples. We
evaluate an RBF kernel, which assumes very smooth variation, and a
Mat\'ern-$3/2$ kernel, which admits rougher functions and retains more
uncertainty between samples. The formulation is otherwise kernel-agnostic;
anisotropic spatial kernels or material-dependent directional kernels can be
introduced without changing the Bayesian-quadrature equations.

We fit kernel length scales by maximising GP marginal likelihood on
observations sampled from each trained map. This adapts the covariance to the
units and characteristic scale of each scene. The fitted kernel determines
the meaning and absolute scale of the reported variance, so uncertainty
values are compared only under a common kernel model.

\section{Experiments}

We test the two effects that \method{} is designed to measure: incomplete
spatial coverage, from the number of primitives available to represent a
scene, and incomplete directional coverage, from missing viewing angles.

\subsection{Spatial coverage}

We train the lego scene at six splat budgets -- $500$; $10{,}000$;
$30{,}000$; $100{,}000$; $300{,}000$ (this project's standard recipe); and
$1{,}000{,}000$ -- and render the same held-out view against each checkpoint.
Held-out PSNR rises from $21.4\,$dB at $500$ splats to $30.5$, $34.9$,
$38.7$, $40.4$, and $41.0\,$dB at $1{,}000{,}000$, with diminishing returns
past roughly $100{,}000$ splats. Posterior variance tracks the same curve
spatially rather than just in aggregate (Figure~\ref{fig:spatial}): at $500$
splats it is elevated across nearly the whole object, and by
$300{,}000$--$1{,}000{,}000$ splats it concentrates onto a shrinking set of
genuinely under-resolved regions instead of shrinking uniformly.

\subsection{Directional coverage}

We construct a real coverage-gap sweep on the lego scene: symmetric angular
windows of increasing half-width ($0^\circ$ to $75^\circ$) are removed from
the 100 training views, and the same held-out view nearest the gap center is
rendered against each condition's own checkpoint. Reconstruction quality
tracks the missing coverage ($35.1$, $29.2$, $22.9$, $15.1$, and
$16.1\,$dB), and the raw rendering-aware posterior variance grows
monotonically with the gap, by roughly two orders of magnitude: mean
variance $0.82 \to 8.54 \to 22.12 \to 59.45 \to 78.85$ (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}).

\section{Discussion}

\method{} measures uncertainty in a numerical representation. Under the GP
model, it is the posterior variance of a local or rendered integral. Under the
corresponding RKHS model, it controls worst-case quadrature error through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:error-bound}. This explains why two neighbourhoods with the same
number of splats can have different uncertainty: their sample arrangements
constrain the integral differently.

The interpretation is conditional on the kernel and rendering functional. A
smooth RBF kernel, for example, does not represent arbitrary discontinuities.
Uniform-box quadrature measures local representation support, whereas the
rendering-aware construction measures support for the alpha-weighted radiance
integral. Neither is, by itself, a calibrated model of every source of image
error. Incorrect geometry, photometric noise, and optimisation failure may
produce error even in densely supported regions.

This boundary distinguishes \method{} from predictive photometric approaches
such as Galappaththige et al.\ \cite{galappaththige2026predictive}. Their
residual-supervised uncertainty is expected to align more directly with held-
out RGB or perceptual error and intentionally combines several error sources.
Our variance isolates finite spatial and directional support and remains
available without fitting an error channel. The two signals could be combined:
quadrature variance provides a structured epistemic prior, while residual
learning captures systematic error that sample geometry alone cannot reveal.

The current implementation solves a dense local GP system for each query,
with cubic complexity in the neighbourhood size. A $k$-d tree, a 400-neighbour
cap, and cached kernel moments make offline analysis practical. Batched
Cholesky solves, incremental factorisations, or sparse GP approximations would
be required for dense real-time maps.

Directional uncertainty also depends on camera--splat attribution. Our
frustum and depth test is an approximation to the true visibility history.
Logging alpha-weighted primitive contributions during training would provide
a cleaner observation set. Finally, high uncertainty does not imply that a
new primitive should be added: empty space is uncertain because it contains
no samples. Densification and exploration must therefore combine uncertainty
with occupancy or visibility.

\section{Conclusion}

We have recast uncertainty in Gaussian-splat maps as a quadrature problem.
Alpha compositing evaluates a rendering integral from a finite set of
primitives; Bayesian quadrature places a posterior over that integral and
quantifies the support supplied by the available samples. This yields a
post-hoc measure of spatial and directional representation uncertainty. A
query-specific kernel derived from the renderer's own alpha-compositing
weights connects the same construction to visible radiance and suppresses
occluded primitives. Experiments show that the variance responds predictably
to sparse spatial coverage and missing viewing directions. \method{} does not attempt to replace a
predictor of photometric error. It answers a prior question: where is the map
being asked to infer more than its finite observations support?
