\section{Introduction}


\section{Background}

\subsection{Rendering with Gaussian splats}
\label{sec:rendering-with-splats}

Neural radiance fields represent density and view-dependent colour as
continuous functions and render pixels by integrating radiance along camera
rays \cite{mildenhall2020}. 3DGS instead represents the scene explicitly as
anisotropic Gaussian primitives \cite{kerbl2023}. Each primitive $i$ has a
centre $\mu_i$, covariance $\Sigma_i$, opacity parameter, and a
view-dependent colour $c_i(d)$, commonly represented by spherical harmonics.

For a pixel $q$, the visible Gaussians are projected to the image plane and
sorted from front to back. If $\alpha_{q,i}$ is the projected opacity of the
$i$th primitive and

\begin{equation}
    T_{q,i}=\prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_{q,j})
\end{equation}

is its accumulated transmittance, the rendered colour is

\begin{equation}
    C(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{m} T_{q,i}\alpha_{q,i}c_i(d_q).
    \label{eq:alpha-compositing}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:alpha-compositing} is a weighted sum, but it is also a
quadrature rule. It evaluates the continuous volume-rendering functional

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_q[c]=\int a_q(\xi)c(\xi,d_q)\,d\xi,
    \label{eq:rendering-functional}
\end{equation}

where $a_q(\xi)$ combines transmittance, opacity or density, and the projected
footprint of the ray. Under the piecewise-constant colour and density model
used in volume rendering, the integral reduces exactly to the ordered
alpha-compositing sum \cite{max1995}. Thus compositing is not merely analogous
to quadrature: it is a particular quadrature rule for the rendering integral,
with nodes supplied by the primitives and weights supplied by the renderer.

\subsection{From quadrature rules to uncertainty}

Consider a general integral

\begin{equation}
    I[f]=\int_{\Omega} f(x)p(x)\,dx,
    \label{eq:integral}
\end{equation}

where $f$ is known only at locations $X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_m\}$. A quadrature
rule approximates this integral by

\begin{equation}
    I[f]\approx \widehat I[f]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_i f(x_i).
    \label{eq:quadrature}
\end{equation}

The nodes $x_i$ determine where the function is evaluated and the weights
$w_i$ determine how those values contribute. Newton--Cotes, Gaussian
quadrature, Monte Carlo integration, and alpha compositing all take this form;
they differ in how they choose nodes and weights. A conventional quadrature
rule returns an estimate but does not, by itself, express what could have
happened between the samples.

Probabilistic numerics treats that missing information as an inference
problem. In Bayesian quadrature \cite{ohagan1991,briol2019}, the unknown
integrand is assigned a Gaussian-process prior,

\begin{equation}
    f\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k),
\end{equation}

where $k$ encodes assumptions about the variation of $f$. Conditioning on
$\mathbf{f}=[f(x_1),\ldots,f(x_m)]^\top$ produces a posterior over functions.
Since integration is linear, the induced posterior over the integral is
Gaussian:

\begin{equation}
    I[f]\mid \mathbf f,X \sim
    \mathcal N\!\left(\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf f,
    z_0-\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf z\right),
    \label{eq:bq-posterior}
\end{equation}

with

\begin{align}
    K_{ij} &= k(x_i,x_j), \\
    z_i &= \int_{\Omega}k(x,x_i)p(x)\,dx, \\
    z_0 &= \int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}
    k(x,x')p(x)p(x')\,dx\,dx'.
\end{align}

The posterior mean is itself a quadrature rule, with weights
$\mathbf w=K^{-1}\mathbf z$. The posterior variance

\begin{equation}
    u(\Omega)=z_0-\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf z
    \label{eq:variance}
\end{equation}

depends on the sample locations, integration measure, and kernel, but not on
the observed function values. It is small when the samples constrain all
functions allowed by the prior over the relevant domain. It is large when
substantially different functions can pass through the same observations yet
produce different integrals. This is the sense in which Bayesian quadrature
provides epistemic uncertainty about a numerical computation.

The same quantity has a deterministic interpretation. For functions in the
reproducing-kernel Hilbert space associated with $k$,

\begin{equation}
    |I[f]-\widehat I[f]|
    \leq \lVert f\rVert_k\sqrt{u(\Omega)}.
    \label{eq:error-bound}
\end{equation}

The posterior standard deviation is therefore also the worst-case integration
error over the unit ball of that function space.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Uncertainty in radiance fields and Gaussian splatting}

Uncertainty methods for radiance fields and 3DGS broadly operate on model
parameters, rendered predictions, or observation coverage. Stochastic
Gaussian Splatting learns distributions over Gaussian parameters using
variational inference \cite{savant2024}. Bayes' Rays applies a Laplace
approximation to a deformation field \cite{goli2024}. FisherRF uses Fisher
information for uncertainty estimation and active view selection
\cite{jiang2024}, while PUP 3D-GS uses second-order sensitivity for pruning
\cite{hanson2025}. GAVIS records directional visibility using spherical
harmonics for active mapping \cite{xue2026}. Mip-Splatting addresses a related
but distinct source of error: aliasing under changes in image sampling rate
\cite{yu2024}.

Galappaththige et al.\ recently proposed a post-hoc method for predictive
photometric uncertainty in 3DGS \cite{galappaththige2026predictive}. Their
method freezes a trained map and learns an additional per-primitive
uncertainty channel from training-view reconstruction residuals. These
channels are represented with spherical harmonics and rendered with the same
alpha-compositing weights as colour. A Gaussian prior, implemented through
$L^2$ regularisation, drives unsupported directions towards a prescribed
maximum uncertainty.


\subsection{Bayesian quadrature for representation analysis}

Bayesian quadrature is usually used when function evaluations are expensive
and uncertainty in an integral matters \cite{ohagan1991,briol2019}. Our use is
slightly different. The function values stored by a trained 3DGS map are
already available; the question is whether their locations provide adequate
support for a new spatial or directional query. The posterior variance is
therefore used as a geometric property of the representation. This turns a
tool for uncertain numerical integration into a post-hoc diagnostic for
finite scene maps.

\section{A Renderer-Consistent View of Gaussian Splat Maps}
\label{sec:method}

Building on Section~\ref{sec:rendering-with-splats}'s observation that
alpha compositing is itself a quadrature rule: for a position-only kernel
and point-mass primitives, the Bayesian-quadrature-\emph{optimal}
solved-for weights $\mathbf w^\ast=K^{-1}\mathbf z$ coincide exactly with
the real compositing weights $w_{q,i}=T_{q,i}\alpha_{q,i}$, for any kernel
bandwidth. That equivalence breaks the moment a directional term is
added to the kernel to say anything about viewing-angle coverage: the
query moment vector evaluates direction against one fixed query direction,
$k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d_i,d_*)$, while the Gram matrix correlates direction
between candidate pairs, $k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d_i,d_j)$. These enter the
linear system in structurally different ways and do not cancel, so the
solved-for weights $\mathbf w^\ast$ no longer track $w_{q,i}$ once
direction is involved --- confirmed directly (exact agreement as
$\kappa\to0$, disagreement of two orders of magnitude at a realistic
$\kappa$). Visually, this appears as chromatic speckle in the
Bayesian-quadrature posterior mean: a directional Bayesian-quadrature
\emph{mean} is a worse image than the alpha-compositing render it is
supposed to refine.

The fix is not a better directional kernel. A splat's stored
spherical-harmonic coefficients are not a point observation of the
radiance field at a location and direction; they are a learned, localized
basis function, and the representation that photometric training actually
optimizes. Treating them as inducing variables of a sparse Gaussian
process --- rather than as point evaluations --- lets the posterior mean
be pinned to the real renderer output \emph{by construction}, so that
uncertainty is purely post hoc and can never corrupt the reconstruction.

\subsection{The renderer as an exact posterior mean}
\label{sec:renderer-consistency}

Fix the splat geometry (centres, covariances, opacities) and, for one
colour channel, let $\theta_i\in\R^{B}$ collect splat $i$'s spherical-
harmonic coefficients, with real SH feature vector $\phi(d)\in\R^{B}$ (the
same basis \texttt{gsplat}/3DGS evaluate a splat's colour against, pulled
out as a design vector rather than evaluated straight through a fixed
coefficient vector). Splat $i$'s colour in direction $d$ is
$c_i(d)=\phi(d)^\top\theta_i$. Writing the real compositing weight as
$\beta_{q,i}=T_{q,i}\alpha_{q,i}$ and stacking every splat's coefficients
into $\theta$, alpha compositing (Eq.~\eqref{eq:alpha-compositing}) is
exactly the linear read-out

\begin{equation}
    C_\alpha(q)=\sum_i\beta_{q,i}\,\phi(d_q)^\top\theta_i
    = b_q^\top\theta,
    \qquad
    b_q^\top=\big[\beta_{q,1}\phi(d_q)^\top,\ldots\big].
    \label{eq:renderer-readout}
\end{equation}

Now let $f(x,d)\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k)$ be an unknown continuous radiance
field, and treat each stored coefficient as an \emph{interdomain inducing
variable} of $f$ --- a linear functional of $f$ localized to splat $i$'s
own spatial support and its $\ell$-th spherical-harmonic mode, rather than
a point evaluation $f(x_i,d_i)$. For a render functional $F_q=\mathcal
L_q[f]$, the joint Gaussian process over the inducing variables $U$ and
$F_q$ gives the standard sparse-GP conditional,
\begin{equation}
    F_q\mid U\sim\mathcal N\big(A_qU,\;u_{\mathrm{rep}}(q)\big),
    \qquad A_q=k_{qU}K_{UU}^{-1},
    \label{eq:sparse-gp-conditional}
\end{equation}
with $u_{\mathrm{rep}}(q)=k_{qq}-k_{qU}K_{UU}^{-1}k_{Uq}$ the usual
generalized-Bayesian-quadrature representation error, now for linear
functionals of the inducing variables rather than point observations.
Choosing the inducing functionals so that $A_q=b_q^\top$ ---
renderer-aligned interdomain inducing variables, or equivalently a
Bayes--Sard construction that makes the SH basis an exactly-integrated
mean space --- and setting $\mathbb E[U]=\hat\theta$ (the coefficients
3DGS actually trained) gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E[F_q]=A_q\hat\theta=b_q^\top\hat\theta=C_\alpha(q).
    \label{eq:mean-is-renderer}
\end{equation}
The posterior mean under this construction is not an approximation to
alpha compositing: it \emph{is} alpha compositing, exactly, for every
query. This is the property the point-evaluation, joint
position--direction kernel described above never delivers once direction
is involved.

\subsection{Two independent uncertainty terms}

The field decomposes as $f=f_{\mathrm{GS}}+f_\perp$, where $f_{\mathrm
{GS}}$ lies in the subspace the finite splat/SH representation spans and
$f_\perp$ is everything else. The rendered pixel is $F_q=b_q^\top U +
\mathcal L_q[f_\perp]$, so the predictive variance splits into the
finite-representation term $u_{\mathrm{rep}}(q)=\mathcal L_q\mathcal
L_q[k_\perp]$ (uncertainty in modes the map does not represent at all)
and a second term from treating the trained coefficients $\hat\theta$ as
estimated, not exact. Approximating $U\mid\mathcal D\approx\mathcal
N(\hat\theta,\Sigma_\theta)$ (a post-hoc Laplace approximation, with
$\Sigma_\theta\approx(\Lambda_0+\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^{-2}J^\top J)^{-1}$
for a linear renderer's Jacobian $J_q=b_q^\top$) and integrating over $U$
gives the complete predictive distribution,
\begin{equation}
    F_q\mid\mathcal D\sim\mathcal N\!\Big(C_\alpha(q),\;
    \underbrace{u_{\mathrm{rep}}(q)}_{\text{spatial}}
    +\underbrace{b_q^\top\Sigma_\theta b_q}_{\text{directional}}
    +\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^2\Big).
    \label{eq:full-predictive}
\end{equation}
We report the first two terms, dropping the constant observation-noise
floor:
\begin{equation}
    u_q = u_{\mathrm{spatial\,BQ}}(q) + u_{\mathrm{SH}}(q).
    \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Spatial term.}
\label{sec:rendering-aware}
$u_{\mathrm{spatial\,BQ}}(q)$ is $u_{\mathrm{rep}}(q)$ under the simplest
point-evaluation inducing variables in position alone (a rendering-aware
kernel $k_q(\xi,\xi')=a_q(\xi)k_{\mathrm{pos}}(\xi,\xi')a_q(\xi')$ for the
query-specific renderer weight $a_q$, with no directional factor at all):
the real compositing weights' own worst-case risk,
\begin{equation}
    u_{\mathrm{spatial\,BQ}}(q)=z_{q,0}-2\,\mathbf w_q^\top\mathbf z_q
    +\mathbf w_q^\top K\mathbf w_q,
    \label{eq:spatial-risk}
\end{equation}
evaluated at the real weights $\mathbf w_q=[\beta_{q,i}]$ rather than
solved for --- no matrix inverse is needed, since Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial-risk}
is a quadratic form in an already-known vector. Because
Eq.~\eqref{eq:spatial-risk} is uniquely minimized at $\mathbf w^\ast=
K^{-1}\mathbf z_q$ (the classical Bayes--Hermite result), it never falls
below the position-only Bayesian-quadrature-optimal variance; scoring the
real weights directly, rather than solving for and reporting a different
estimator's variance, is what keeps this term paired with the mean it is
actually computed for.

\paragraph{Directional term.} $\Sigma_\theta$ is block-diagonal across
splats under an independence approximation, so $u_{\mathrm{SH}}(q)=
\sum_i\beta_{q,i}^2\,\phi(d_q)^\top\Sigma_{\theta_i}\phi(d_q)$, with each
splat's own coefficient posterior a per-splat Bayesian linear regression
over its real observed training directions $\mathcal D_i$,
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_{\theta_i}^{-1}=\lambda I+\sum_{p\in\mathcal D_i}
    \beta_{p,i}^2\,\phi(d_p)\phi(d_p)^\top,
    \label{eq:sh-precision}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_{p,i}$ is splat $i$'s own real alpha-compositing weight in
training observation $p$ --- how much it actually contributed to whatever
pixel its own projected centre landed on in that training camera, not a
binary visible/occluded flag. We obtain every $\beta_{p,i}$ by
rerendering the fixed training cameras (no retraining): querying at each
splat's own projected bearing in each camera it plausibly observed, and
taking its own slot in the real depth-ordered alpha-compositing
transmittance weights along that ray. Crucially, $\Sigma_{\theta_i}$ does
not depend on observed \emph{colours} at all, only on which directions
were observed and how strongly each observation weighted that splat --- a
Fisher-information/coverage statistic, which is exactly the quantity the
earlier point-evaluation directional kernel (Section~\ref{sec:method})
was structurally unable to express correctly. A splat seen from many
angularly diverse, strongly-weighted directions has small
$\Sigma_{\theta_i}$ everywhere; one seen from a narrow cone, or only ever
weakly, stays uncertain away from that cone.

\subsection{Kernel choice and fitting}
\label{sec:kernel-choice}

The position kernel $k_{\mathrm{pos}}$ is an RBF, with bandwidth fit per
checkpoint by maximizing GP marginal likelihood on local (position,
colour) windows sampled from the trained map, jointly with a real
homoscedastic observation-noise variance (real splat positions routinely
include near-duplicate points, which forces a noiseless fit toward an
artificially short bandwidth just to keep exactly interpolating through
them). The SH-coefficient prior precision $\lambda$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sh-precision} is currently a fixed scalar rather than
fit from data; a marginal-likelihood fit for it, and a quantitative
calibration check of $u_q$ against real held-out rendering error, are
open (see the project roadmap).

\section{Using the Uncertainty}

\subsection{Uncertainty maps}

We form uncertainty images by evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:decomposition} at
every real ray--surface hit in a held-out view (depth-unprojected from
\texttt{gsplat}'s own expected-depth output), reporting $u_{\mathrm{spatial\,
BQ}}$, $u_{\mathrm{SH}}$, and their sum alongside the renderer's own
output $C_\alpha(q)$ --- never a Bayesian-quadrature-solved mean.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We test the property the renderer-consistent construction is designed to
guarantee, and the qualitative behaviour of the two uncertainty terms it
produces, on three real NeRF-Synthetic scenes (lego, chair, ship; one
held-out view each, 300{,}000-splat checkpoints; Figure~\ref{fig:sparse-gp}).

\subsection{The mean never departs from the renderer}

$C_\alpha(q)$ is, by construction, the real \texttt{gsplat} alpha-composited
output --- not a quantity solved for under a Bayesian-quadrature posterior.
On all three scenes the rendered mean column is visually identical to
the ground-truth reconstruction, with none of the chromatic speckle the
earlier directional point-evaluation kernel produced wherever it was
asked to solve for a directionally-aware posterior mean
(Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\subsection{The two uncertainty terms are visibly distinct}

$u_{\mathrm{spatial\,BQ}}$ is sharp and structure-following on every
scene, concentrating on fine geometric detail --- edges, thin structures
such as the ship's rigging --- consistent with it measuring finite
spatial support. $u_{\mathrm{SH}}$ is smoother and more spatially
coherent, consistent with it tracking real training-view angular
coverage, a property that varies more smoothly across a surface than
per-splat spatial density does. The two terms are not redundant: regions
of high $u_{\mathrm{spatial\,BQ}}$ do not coincide exactly with regions of
high $u_{\mathrm{SH}}$, and the sum visibly combines both --- a spatial
question and a directional question, answerable independently, as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:decomposition} intends.

Rerendering every real training camera to build $\Sigma_{\theta_i}$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:sh-precision}) took 8--11 minutes per 300{,}000-splat
scene; both query-side terms evaluate in under a second per view once
$\Sigma_\theta$ is built. A quantitative calibration check of $u_q$
against real held-out rendering error, and a marginal-likelihood fit for
$\lambda$, are left to future work rather than assumed from the
qualitative result above.
