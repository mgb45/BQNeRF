\begin{abstract}
Three-dimensional Gaussian splatting can reconstruct and render a scene, but
does not tell us where that reconstruction is well supported. This matters in
robotics, where a map is used to decide where to move, what to observe, or
which parts of a representation can be discarded. We introduce \method{}, a
post-hoc uncertainty measure for trained Gaussian-splat maps. The method views
the splats near a query as samples of an unknown local scene function and uses
Bayesian quadrature to quantify how well those samples determine a local
integral. The resulting variance depends on the locations of the splats and,
when required, the directions from which they were observed. It requires no
ensemble, additional uncertainty network, or retraining. On the eight
NeRF-Synthetic scenes, spatial uncertainty is strongly anticorrelated with
local splat density, with Pearson correlations from $-0.97$ to $-0.74$.
Controlled camera-coverage experiments show that directional uncertainty
increases monotonically as viewing directions are removed. We also derive a
renderer-aware refinement that replaces the uniform integration measure with
the splats' own alpha-compositing weights, which ties the reported variance
provably to alpha-compositing error and, on real checkpoints, correctly
down-weights occluded splats and disentangles spatial from directional
support. We also use the box-quadrature signal for uncertainty-aware pruning
and next-view selection. \method{}
provides a useful measure of representation uncertainty: it identifies where
a map is weakly supported, although it is not a calibrated predictor of pixel
error.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A map is not only an estimate of the world. It should also tell us where that
estimate is likely to fail. This is particularly important for learned maps.
A novel view rendered from a three-dimensional Gaussian-splat (3DGS) model may
look plausible even when it is supported by few primitives or by observations
from a narrow range of directions. The renderer returns a colour in either
case. A robot using the map receives no corresponding warning.

This is an uncertainty-quantification problem. We want to distinguish regions
that are well constrained by the representation from regions in which the map
must interpolate---or extrapolate---from limited evidence. Existing methods
estimate this uncertainty using distributions over model parameters,
ensembles, local curvature, or learned visibility fields
\cite{savant2024,goli2024,jiang2024,hanson2025,xue2026}. These methods can be
effective, but typically change training, add learned state, or require
second-order information.

We instead ask a simpler question: how well do the splats in an existing map
support a local query? A trained 3DGS map represents a continuous scene using
a finite set of Gaussian primitives. Any local estimate is therefore based on
samples that cover some parts of position--view space better than others.
Bayesian quadrature provides a natural way to quantify this coverage. It
places a Gaussian-process prior on an unknown function and induces a posterior
distribution over its integral. Importantly, the posterior variance depends
on where observations were made. It is small when the integration domain is
well covered and large where the available samples leave plausible functions
unconstrained.

We apply this idea to the primitives already stored in a Gaussian-splat map.
For a spatial query, the sample set consists of nearby splat centres. For a
view-dependent query, each sample also includes a direction from which the
splat was observed. A product kernel then measures coverage jointly in
position and direction. The result is a post-hoc estimate of
\emph{representation uncertainty}: uncertainty arising from finite spatial
and directional support in the map.

This interpretation is deliberately narrower than predictive uncertainty.
The proposed variance does not model every source of rendering error, such as
incorrect geometry, optimisation failure, occlusion mistakes, or photometric
noise. In its simplest, uniform-box form the quadrature measure also does not
coincide with the alpha-compositing weights 3DGS itself uses to render a
pixel. We additionally develop a renderer-aware form of the same construction
that replaces the uniform measure with the splats' own alpha-compositing
weights and is provably tied to alpha-compositing error, but this refinement
is validated separately from our main post-hoc results and is not yet what
drives them. Bayesian quadrature is used here to evaluate the support
provided by the splat locations, not to replace the renderer.

The paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate spatial support in a trained Gaussian-splat map as a
    Bayesian-quadrature uncertainty problem.
    \item We extend the same construction to joint position--direction space,
    separating missing geometric support from missing view coverage.
    \item We derive a renderer-aware refinement that replaces the uniform
    integration measure with a query-specific alpha-compositing weight,
    proving that its variance bounds alpha-compositing error and confirming
    empirically that it down-weights occluded splats and correctly separates
    spatial from directional support.
    \item We show that the resulting uncertainty can be computed post-hoc and
    used to analyse a map, prune splats, and select new camera views.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Gaussian splatting and map uncertainty}

Neural radiance fields represent density and view-dependent colour as a
continuous function and render pixels by integrating along camera rays
\cite{mildenhall2020}. 3DGS instead represents a scene using explicit,
anisotropic Gaussian primitives and renders them using ordered alpha
compositing \cite{kerbl2023}. Its explicit representation is fast to render
and easy to query, which makes it attractive for mapping and active
perception. Standard 3DGS, however, provides no measure of confidence in a
rendered view.

Several recent methods add such a measure. Stochastic Gaussian Splatting uses
variational inference to learn distributions over Gaussian parameters
\cite{savant2024}. Bayes' Rays applies a Laplace approximation to a
hypothetical deformation field \cite{goli2024}. FisherRF uses Fisher
information for uncertainty estimation and active view selection
\cite{jiang2024}, while PUP 3D-GS uses second-order sensitivity for pruning
\cite{hanson2025}. GAVIS records directional visibility using spherical
harmonics and uses this representation for active mapping \cite{xue2026}.
Mip-Splatting addresses a related but different question: aliasing caused by
changes in image sampling rate \cite{yu2024}.

These approaches quantify parameter sensitivity, learn a distribution, or
store additional visibility information. Our interest is complementary. We
measure whether the existing primitives adequately cover the domain of a
query.

\subsection{Bayesian quadrature as uncertainty quantification}

Many quantities in science and engineering are integrals,
\begin{equation}
    I[f] = \int_{\Omega} f(x)p(x)\,dx,
    \label{eq:integral}
\end{equation}
but the function $f$ can usually be evaluated at only finitely many locations
$X=\{x_1,\ldots,x_m\}$. Standard numerical quadrature approximates the
integral with a weighted sum,
\begin{equation}
    I[f] \approx \widehat I[f] = \sum_{i=1}^{m} w_i f(x_i).
    \label{eq:quadrature}
\end{equation}
The estimate alone does not say how much information was lost between the
samples.

Bayesian quadrature turns this missing information into an uncertainty model
\cite{ohagan1991,briol2019}. We place a Gaussian-process prior on the unknown
integrand,
\begin{equation}
    f \sim \mathcal{GP}(0,k),
\end{equation}
where the kernel $k$ encodes how quickly the function is expected to vary.
Conditioning the process on the observed values
$\mathbf f=[f(x_1),\ldots,f(x_m)]^\top$ gives a posterior over functions.
Because integration is a linear operation, this also gives a Gaussian
posterior over the integral,
\begin{equation}
    I[f]\mid \mathbf f,X \sim
    \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf f,
    z_0-\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf z\right),
    \label{eq:bq-posterior}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
    K_{ij} &= k(x_i,x_j), \\
    z_i &= \int_{\Omega} k(x,x_i)p(x)\,dx, \\
    z_0 &= \int_{\Omega}\!\int_{\Omega}
    k(x,x')p(x)p(x')\,dx\,dx'.
\end{align}

The posterior mean in Eq.~\eqref{eq:bq-posterior} is a quadrature rule. The
posterior variance,
\begin{equation}
    u(\Omega)=z_0-\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf z,
    \label{eq:variance}
\end{equation}
quantifies what remains unknown about the integral after observing the
function at $X$. Unlike a residual, this variance does not depend on the
observed values $\mathbf f$. It depends on the sample locations, the
integration domain, and the kernel. It is therefore a measure of
\emph{epistemic} uncertainty: uncertainty caused by limited information.

This is useful for map analysis. Dense, well-distributed samples reduce the
variance. Sparse or clustered samples leave functions that agree at the
observed locations but differ elsewhere, so the integral remains uncertain.
Bayesian quadrature therefore measures more than sample count: it measures
how informative the arrangement of samples is under the assumed kernel.

\section{Bayesian Quadrature for Gaussian Splat Maps}

\subsection{Spatial support}

Consider a query region $\Omega\subset\R^3$ and the $m$ Gaussian centres near
that region. We treat these centres as the locations at which the underlying
scene representation has been sampled. Applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:variance}
gives a scalar measure of how well the splats cover $\Omega$.

The interpretation is straightforward. The prior term $z_0$ is the
uncertainty in the local integral before observing any splats. The reduction
$\mathbf z^\top K^{-1}\mathbf z$ is the information supplied by the splat
centres. A region containing well-placed nearby splats has low uncertainty. A
region with few splats, or with splats concentrated on only one part of the
domain, has high uncertainty.

The splat colours do not appear in this calculation. \method{} is therefore
not a photometric error estimator. It measures whether the representation
supports the query. This makes the uncertainty available after training using
only the structure of the map.

We use a small axis-aligned box centred on each query point as $\Omega$. Nearby
splats are retrieved using a $k$-d tree, and each solve uses at most 400
neighbours. The integration density $p(x)$ is uniform within the box. For
separable kernels, the terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:bq-posterior} reduce to products
of one-dimensional kernel integrals.

\subsection{Directional support}
\label{sec:directional-support}

Spatial support is not sufficient for view-dependent appearance. A surface
may be represented by many splats but observed from only one side. Rendering
that surface from a new direction is then an extrapolation in viewing angle.

We represent each observation by a position--direction pair $(x_i,d_i)$,
where $d_i$ is a unit viewing direction, and use the product kernel
\begin{equation}
    k\big((x,d),(x',d')\big)
    =k_{\mathrm{pos}}(x,x')k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d,d').
    \label{eq:product-kernel}
\end{equation}
The directional component is
\begin{equation}
    k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d,d')
    =\exp\!\left(\kappa(d^\top d'-1)\right),
    \label{eq:direction-kernel}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ determines how quickly information decays with angular
separation. A small $\kappa$ shares information over a broad range of
directions. A large $\kappa$ assumes stronger view dependence.

For a query direction $d_*$, the kernel mean becomes
\begin{equation}
    z_i=\left[\int_{\Omega}k_{\mathrm{pos}}(x,x_i)p(x)\,dx\right]
    k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d_i,d_*).
    \label{eq:directional-mean}
\end{equation}
The variance is now low only when the query is supported in both position and
direction. Position-only and position--direction uncertainty are therefore
two instances of the same model, obtained by choosing the domain and kernel
appropriate to the question.

The training process does not retain a complete record of which cameras
contributed to each splat. We recover these associations using camera frusta
and a soft angular depth test, then attach the corresponding viewing
directions to each visible splat.

\subsection{Rendering-aware quadrature}
\label{sec:rendering-aware}

Both constructions above integrate the base kernel uniformly over an
axis-aligned box: every splat inside the box is weighted only by where it
sits relative to the box, never by how much it actually contributes to a
rendered pixel. We now replace that uniform measure with a query-specific
weight tied to the renderer itself.

For a query $q$ (a pixel or ray), define
\begin{equation}
    a_q(\xi) = T_q(\xi)\,\sigma(\xi)\,G_q(\xi),
    \label{eq:render-weight}
\end{equation}
the product of accumulated transmittance, density/opacity, and a
footprint/visibility term---exactly the quantities standard alpha
compositing already combines at each sample $\xi$. Placing a GP prior on the
radiance field $c(\xi)$ rather than on the rendering integrand
$a_q(\xi)c(\xi)$ makes the kernel itself query-specific,
\begin{equation}
    k_q(\xi,\xi') = a_q(\xi)\,k_{\mathrm{base}}(\xi,\xi')\,a_q(\xi'),
    \label{eq:render-kernel}
\end{equation}
with the moments in Eq.~\eqref{eq:bq-posterior} replaced by
\begin{align}
    z_{q,i} &= \int a_q(\xi)\,k_{\mathrm{base}}(\xi,x_i)\,d\xi, \\
    z_{q,0} &= \int\!\int a_q(\xi)a_q(\xi')\,k_{\mathrm{base}}(\xi,\xi')\,d\xi\,d\xi'.
\end{align}
The Gram matrix $K$ is unchanged---observations are noiseless colours
$c(x_i)$, not $a_q(x_i)c(x_i)$---so only the moment vector and prior variance
become renderer-aware. We model $a_q$ as an unnormalised Gaussian bump
(amplitude, centre, covariance); for an isotropic RBF $k_{\mathrm{base}}$
this makes $z_{q,i}$ and $z_{q,0}$ available in closed form via the standard
Gaussian-product identity, with a numerical fallback for other kernel
families.

This is more than a modelling convenience. Under the piecewise-constant
density/colour model every NeRF- and 3DGS-style renderer already assumes,
the continuous rendering integral is \emph{exactly} the discrete
alpha-compositing sum, not an approximation of it \cite{max1995}. Bayesian
quadrature's posterior mean is the RKHS-optimal linear estimator of that same
integral, and its posterior variance is a provable worst-case bound on the
estimator's error \cite{ohagan1991,briol2019}. Replacing the uniform box
measure with $a_q$ therefore ties the reported variance to error against the
renderer's own alpha-compositing value, rather than against an integral
defined over an arbitrary window.

We build $a_q$ from real data at three levels of fidelity. The simplest uses
each neighbourhood's mean per-splat opacity as $a_q$'s amplitude with a
Gaussian footprint tied to the query radius; this is occlusion-blind. The
next replaces it with the real, depth-ordered alpha-compositing
transmittance along the specific camera ray through the query point,
computed from real per-splat opacity via the standard compositing
recursion. The most faithful uses gsplat's own differentiable EWA-splatting
projection to obtain each splat's real anisotropic 2-D footprint and exact
per-pixel alpha. In every case the resulting nonnegative per-neighbour
compositing weights are moment-matched to a single Gaussian $a_q$: its
centre and covariance come from the weighted mean and spread of the
contributing splats (plus their own real covariances, for the anisotropic
case), and its total mass is pinned to the sum of the compositing weights,
which is exactly $1-T_{\mathrm{final}}$ for real alpha compositing---a
bounded quantity---rather than to an arbitrary peak amplitude, whose scale
would otherwise depend on an unrelated footprint volume.

The directional extension of Section~\ref{sec:directional-support} composes
directly with this construction. $a_q$ remains position-only---rendering
weight concerns \emph{where} mass concentrates along a ray, not which
direction a splat happens to have been observed from---so only the base
kernel becomes joint, $k_{\mathrm{base}}(\xi,\xi')=k_{\mathrm{pos}}(x,x')
k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d,d')$, giving
\begin{equation}
    z_{q,i} = \left[\int a_q(\xi)k_{\mathrm{pos}}(\xi,x_i)\,d\xi\right]
    k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d_i,d_*),
    \label{eq:render-directional-mean}
\end{equation}
with $z_{q,0}$ unchanged from the position-only case, since
$k_{\mathrm{dir}}(d,d)=1$ always.

\subsection{Kernel choice and fitting}

The kernel specifies which functions are considered plausible between
samples. We evaluate two spatial kernels. The radial-basis-function (RBF)
kernel assumes very smooth variation and shares information strongly between
nearby locations. The Mat\'ern-$3/2$ kernel admits rougher functions and is
therefore more conservative between samples.

Kernel length scale is as important as kernel family. A short length scale
treats nearby splats as weakly related and produces localised uncertainty. A
long length scale shares information more broadly. We fit length scales by
maximising GP marginal likelihood on observations sampled from each trained
map. This adapts the uncertainty model to the units and spatial scale of the
scene. The formulation is otherwise kernel-agnostic: anisotropic, periodic,
or task-specific kernels can be substituted without changing
Eq.~\eqref{eq:variance}.

\section{Using the Uncertainty}

\subsection{Uncertainty maps}

We obtain an uncertainty image by associating each rendered image region with
its contributing 3-D neighbourhood and evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:variance}.
Spatial uncertainty highlights regions with weak geometric support.
Position--direction uncertainty additionally highlights surfaces observed
from directions unlike the requested view.

\subsection{Uncertainty-aware pruning}

Opacity-based pruning removes weakly contributing primitives, but does not
distinguish redundancy from scarcity. Removing one of many nearby splats is
usually harmless; removing the only splat supporting a thin structure is not.
We therefore combine opacity with spatial uncertainty, assigning greater
value to primitives in poorly supported occupied regions. An opacity floor
excludes empty space, which has high quadrature variance but contains nothing
to preserve.

\subsection{Next-view selection}

Directional uncertainty can also guide data collection. For each candidate
camera pose, we evaluate position--direction uncertainty over its visible
regions and aggregate the values using a visibility or occupancy weight. We
select the camera with the largest score. This favours views of scene content
that is spatially present but poorly observed from the candidate direction,
without retraining the map for each candidate.

\section{Experiments}

We test whether \method{} responds to the two sources of missing support it is
designed to measure: sparse spatial coverage and incomplete directional
coverage. We then examine sensitivity to the kernel and evaluate pruning and
next-view selection as downstream uses. Sections~\ref{sec:spatial-coverage}--
\ref{sec:pruning-nbv} use the uniform-box quadrature of
Section~\ref{sec:directional-support} and earlier; Section~\ref{sec:rendering-aware-exp}
separately validates the renderer-aware refinement of
Section~\ref{sec:rendering-aware}.

\subsection{Spatial coverage}
\label{sec:spatial-coverage}

We train Gaussian-splat maps for all eight NeRF-Synthetic scenes
\cite{mildenhall2020}: chair, drums, ficus, hotdog, lego, materials, mic, and
ship. We retain splats with opacity above $0.1$ and sample 150 query locations
per scene. At each location, we compare position-only uncertainty with the
number of splats in the same neighbourhood.

Uncertainty is strongly anticorrelated with local splat count in every scene.
Pearson $r$ ranges from $-0.97$ to $-0.74$. In the lego scene, the sparsest
$20\%$ of queries have $3.49\times$ the mean variance of the densest $20\%$.
The relationship is also present in a checkpoint trained using the reference
\texttt{gsplat} densification strategy ($r=-0.92$).

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/sparsity_correlation.png}
    \caption{Position-only uncertainty against local splat count in the chair
    scene. Poorly supported regions have higher quadrature uncertainty.}
    \label{fig:spatial}
\end{figure}

Sample count alone does not determine the variance. We train reconstructions
from 100, 50, 25, and 12 randomly selected images, and from 12 images confined
to a narrow angular range. The random subsets produce similar uncertainty,
while the angularly clustered set has $2.75\times$ the uncertainty of the
random 12-view set. The arrangement of the resulting splats therefore matters,
as expected from Eq.~\eqref{eq:variance}.

\subsection{Directional coverage}

We first isolate directional coverage in a controlled scene containing five
geometrically identical regions. Each region is observed over a camera arc
with half-width between $8^\circ$ and $180^\circ$. Position--direction
uncertainty decreases monotonically as the arc widens, with Spearman
$\rho=1.00$ and a $12.97\times$ range. Position-only uncertainty varies by
only $1.76\times$ and has no monotonic trend.

We next remove symmetric angular windows from the 100 training views of the
lego scene. As the gap half-width increases from $0^\circ$ to $75^\circ$,
directional uncertainty increases monotonically ($\rho=1.00$) over a
$6.95\times$ range. Position-only uncertainty changes by $1.34\times$. Within
the model with the largest gap, uncertainty is $14.78$ in the missing
direction and $6.98$ in the opposite, observed direction.

\subsection{Kernel sensitivity}

RBF and Mat\'ern-$3/2$ kernels identify similar spatial patterns: their
uncertainty maps have correlations from $0.98$ to $0.995$. Their absolute
scales differ by roughly $150\times$, since the rougher Mat\'ern prior retains
more uncertainty between samples. After fitting each kernel separately, RBF
uncertainty correlates more strongly with spatial density and gives lower
Gaussian negative log likelihood. Mat\'ern-$3/2$ gives better ranking-based
calibration. Kernel choice therefore changes the meaning and scale of the
uncertainty, even when the spatial ranking is similar.

\subsection{Rendering-aware kernel}
\label{sec:rendering-aware-exp}

We validate the construction of Section~\ref{sec:rendering-aware} directly,
rather than folding it into the multi-scene statistics above.

\emph{Occlusion sensitivity.} On a synthetic ray/pixel scene with an explicit
density and colour field, we place one splat behind an opaque surface and
give it a deliberately wrong colour. Against the true integral of $0.048$,
adding this splat shifts the uniform-box posterior mean by $1.49$ ($\sim
30\%$ of the true value); it shifts the renderer-aware posterior mean by
only $0.0001$ ($\sim 0.2\%$), a $\sim 13{,}500\times$ smaller change, because
$a_q$ is correctly near zero at the occluded splat's depth. On real
300k-splat checkpoints (chair, drums, hotdog; held-out views), a fully
opaque occluder analogously pulls the renderer-aware posterior mean onto its
own colour rather than the occluded target's, both via ray-transmittance
weights and via gsplat's real anisotropic per-pixel alpha.

\emph{Coupling with directional support.} We sweep a camera through a
designed $150^\circ$ training-coverage gap on the lego scene. The
position-only renderer-aware variance is \emph{anti-correlated} with true
coverage (Spearman $\rho=-0.52$ against the position--direction tool of
Section~\ref{sec:directional-support} over the same orbit): a spatially
consistent local colour field says nothing about whether the queried viewing
direction was ever observed. Extending $a_q$ with the joint
position--direction kernel of Eq.~\eqref{eq:render-directional-mean},
computed from $373{,}387$ real (splat, observing-camera) observation rows,
restores agreement with the independently derived directional signal
($\rho=0.97$).

These checks confirm that the renderer-aware kernel behaves as
Section~\ref{sec:rendering-aware} predicts---down-weighting occluded splats
and requiring both spatial and directional support to report low
uncertainty---on real checkpoints, not only in the toy scene. They do not
show that it changes the sparsity-correlation, calibration, pruning, or
next-view-selection numbers above; that comparison is left to future work.

\subsection{Pruning and next-view selection}
\label{sec:pruning-nbv}

We evaluate pruning on a designed scene across several splat budgets. At the
tightest budget, uncertainty-aware pruning improves reconstruction PSNR by
$2.3$ dB over opacity-only pruning. The methods converge at larger budgets,
where both retain the primitives required to represent the scene.

For next-view selection, we score a discrete set of candidate cameras using
position--direction uncertainty and visibility. We add the highest-scoring
view to the training set, retrain the map, and compare it with a
low-scoring-view baseline. The selected view improves held-out PSNR by
$1.89$ dB, compared with $0.65$ dB for the low-scoring view.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What does the variance measure?}

Bayesian-quadrature variance has both a probabilistic and a deterministic
interpretation. Under the GP model it is the posterior variance of the local
integral. For functions in the reproducing-kernel Hilbert space associated
with $k$, it also controls the worst-case integration error,
\begin{equation}
    \left|I[f]-\widehat I[f]\right|
    \leq \lVert f\rVert_k\sqrt{u(\Omega)}.
    \label{eq:error-bound}
\end{equation}
Two neighbourhoods containing the same number of splats can therefore have
different uncertainty when one arrangement covers the region more
effectively.

The guarantee is conditional on the chosen kernel and on the integral being
evaluated. A smooth RBF kernel, for example, is a poor model for arbitrary
discontinuities. More importantly, the uniform-box local 3-D integral used
for the results above is not identical to the depth-ordered colour integral
used to render a pixel; the proposed variance should therefore be read as
uncertainty in the support of the map, not as a confidence interval for RGB
prediction. Section~\ref{sec:rendering-aware} narrows, but does not close,
this gap: replacing the uniform measure with the splats' own alpha-compositing
weights ties the reported variance to the exact rendering integral under the
piecewise-constant model every 3DGS renderer already assumes, but our
construction still lacks antialiasing, sub-pixel footprint integration, and a
gradient path, and is not proven equivalent to a full tile-based rasterizer's
compositing order.

This distinction is visible empirically. Leave-one-out tests on splat colours
show weak correlation between the variance and squared colour error
($|r|<0.21$), and its Gaussian negative log likelihood is worse than a
constant-variance baseline on three tested checkpoints. In contrast, the
variance consistently ranks sparse regions and responds to missing viewing
directions. Its present use is therefore to compare queries and guide map
operations, rather than to predict an absolute probability of rendering
failure.

\subsection{Computational and modelling limits}

Each local query requires a dense linear solve, with cubic complexity in the
number of neighbours. A $k$-d tree, the 400-neighbour cap, and cached prior
integrals make post-hoc analysis practical, but real-time uncertainty images
will require batched Cholesky solves, incremental factorisations, or sparse GP
approximations.

Directional uncertainty also depends on the recovered camera--splat
associations. The frustum-and-depth-test attribution used for the results in
Section~\ref{sec:spatial-coverage}--\ref{sec:pruning-nbv} is a binary, occlusion-
coarse proxy; Section~\ref{sec:rendering-aware}'s real, depth-ordered
transmittance weights are a strictly better per-observation signal and could
replace it, though logging alpha-weighted contributions during training would
still be preferable to any post-hoc reconstruction. Material-dependent
directional kernels would further allow diffuse and specular regions to use
different angular length scales.

Finally, high quadrature uncertainty does not imply that a Gaussian should be
added. Empty space is uncertain precisely because it contains no samples.
Occupancy or visibility is therefore required when using the signal for
densification or exploration.

\section{Conclusion}

\method{} uses Bayesian quadrature to quantify how well the primitives in a
trained Gaussian-splat map support a query. The method is post-hoc, requires
no additional learned uncertainty model, and treats spatial and directional
coverage within one kernel framework. Across eight synthetic scenes, the
variance reliably identifies sparse spatial support. It also responds
monotonically to controlled gaps in camera direction, and can be used to
guide pruning and view selection. We further show that replacing the uniform
integration measure with the splats' own alpha-compositing weights ties the
same posterior variance provably to alpha-compositing error, and confirm on
real checkpoints that this renderer-aware kernel down-weights occluded splats
and correctly separates spatial from directional support; connecting it to
the multi-scene statistics above is left to future work. The method does not
provide calibrated pixel-error probabilities. It provides something narrower
and useful: a principled measure of where a finite scene representation is
being asked to say more than its samples support.
